# Integrated Performance Review: From Market Demand to Factory Execution for El Sharnouby

## Executive Summary
Revenue is growing, but growth is not converting into healthy profit. Total revenue reached 142.83M in 2025, up 6.20% year over year, yet gross margin was only 15.38%, leaving gross profit of 21.97M. Discounts totaled 60.61M, close to 30% of gross sales. Across all four years, the pattern is the same: 421.34M in revenue, a 15.40% margin, and 178.75M in discounts. The discount rate has stayed steady, which makes this a structural issue rather than a one-year event.

Margin is weakest in the channel that drives most of the volume. Wholesale delivers 85% of revenue at an 11.83% margin, while online and retail earn above 35%.

On the operations side, the factory is consistently producing about 91% of its plan, both in 2026 and across all periods. Quality losses reached 3.85M in cost of poor quality, led by stitch defects and size variance. Machine downtime totaled 1,340.6 hours in 2026, and setup and mechanical faults account for almost half of it.

Inventory is unevenly matched to demand. A few slow-moving SKUs hold stock for well over a year, while most of the range is within normal cover. Retention is also weakening, which adds pressure on the business to convert each customer into repeat revenue.

The priorities are to stop margin leakage from blanket discounting, improve wholesale pricing and margin discipline, reduce quality and changeover losses on the factory floor, and tie purchasing to the demand forecast. Marketing spend should be reviewed only after attribution is validated.

## 1. Profit Leakage in Sales
In 2025, the company generated 142.83M in revenue and 21.97M in gross profit. Discounts of 60.61M were applied, and Pajamas/Homewear absorbed roughly two-thirds of them, about 39M. That category also generates the largest share of revenue, roughly 90M or 63% of the total. Across all four years, Pajamas/Homewear carries about two-thirds of discounts, confirming a persistent pattern.

Discounts are concentrated on the category that already sells best. Rather than building loyalty, the promotions give away margin where demand is already strong. Monthly revenue peaks in December and bottoms in February, and September and October run below the seasonal baseline. Promotions are not clearly timed to these weak months, which suggests discounting is not doing the work it appears to do.

> **Immediate action:** Halt blanket discounting on homewear. Replace it with promotions gated by net-margin thresholds, and measure each promotion by its contribution to gross profit, not revenue.

## 2. Channel Economics: Volume Versus Value

| Channel | 2025 revenue | Share of revenue | Gross margin | YoY growth | Gross profit (approx.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Wholesale** | 121.88M | 85.33% | 11.83% | 5.41% | 14M |
| **Online** | 11.18M | 7.83% | 35.14% | 28.44% | 4M |
| **Retail** | 9.77M | 6.84% | 37.05% | -3.91% | 4M |

Wholesale generates about 64% of gross profit on 85% of revenue, but its margin is less than a third of the other channels'. Online is the fastest-growing channel, up 28% year over year, yet contributes under 8% of revenue. Retail is shrinking.

Wholesale margin is the largest single profit lever in the business. Each point of margin improvement there is worth roughly 1.2M in gross profit at current volume. Revenue is also concentrated geographically, with one market dominating the revenue map, which adds regional exposure.

> **Immediate action:** Review wholesale pricing and discount terms with the largest accounts, and set a margin floor for wholesale orders. Build a plan to grow online, which is already growing fastest and earns the highest margin.

## 3. Customer Retention and Value
Customer value is concentrated. VIP customers combine high purchase frequency with high lifetime spend, while a larger group of regular customers buys less often and spends less. Average order value is about 2.34K.

Retention is the weak point. The retention curve falls from about 95% for the November 2023 cohort to about 34% by March 2025. The reported repeat rate on the KPI card is 84.29%, but the repeat-rate trend line sits near 20% in early 2025. These two figures cannot both be right under the same definition, so the report should not cite either until the definition is confirmed. Churn is reported at 15.71%.

Twelve high-value customers on the at-risk list have combined lifetime spend of about 5.9M and an average recency of 91 days. The largest of them has spent about 825K and has not purchased in 115 days.

> **Immediate action:** Launch a win-back program starting with the twelve at-risk accounts. Confirm the repeat-rate and churn definitions before setting retention targets.

## 4. Inventory and Demand Planning
The inventory analysis uses the September 2026 snapshot, the latest month in the data. Across 177 product-location records and 59 products, closing stock is 77,867 units. Portfolio days of cover is about 45, calculated as closing stock divided by the 90-day average daily sales rate. Individual SKUs vary widely: the median record holds about 78 days, while the mean of 361 is pulled up by a small number of very slow sellers. Forty of the 177 records exceed 180 days of cover.

Annualized stock turns are 8.6 at the portfolio level. SKU-level turns and cover use different demand bases, so individual figures will not reconcile exactly, and the report should state this.

Three SKUs are clearly overstocked: Boys' Autumn Pajamas-Jurassic Park (about 1,600 days of cover), Boys' summer pajamas-Seeds OF (about 780 days), and Boys' Autumn Pajamas-Loony Tunes (about 230 days). Jurassic Park and Seeds OF turn only about once a year or less, which ties up working capital in products that are not selling. The overstock card shows four SKUs, and the fourth should be confirmed before the count is quoted.

No SKU in the current view is in stockout territory, but several are close. Boys' Bluey, Boys' pajamas-Don't Even, and Boys' summer pajamas-Mickey have 20 to 25 days of cover and should be reordered soon. Replenishment is the priority for these fast movers.

Supplier lead time averages 11.35 days. A 14-day cover threshold therefore leaves little margin, which supports reordering at roughly the 30-day mark.

The 90-day revenue forecast uses Power BI's built-in exponential smoothing with a 365-point seasonal cycle and a 95% confidence interval. Because the series covers about three years, the seasonal estimate rests on limited data, and the confidence band is wide. The forecast is useful for direction, not for precise purchasing quantities.

> **Immediate action:** Reorder the three fast movers now. Review purchasing for the overstocked SKUs, and consider clearance or range reduction for the slowest sellers. Use the forecast to set purchasing direction, and revisit the quantities once the seasonal estimate is validated.

## 5. Marketing Effectiveness and Attribution
Across all periods, campaign spend totals about 15M. Reported blended ROAS is 12.32, with a 4.17% conversion rate and a 3.10% click-through rate. Attributed revenue by platform is as follows: Google about 40M on 4M spend, Meta about 24M on 5M, TikTok about 11M on 2M, WhatsApp/CRM about 104M on 2M, and influencers about 2M on 1M.

Attributed campaign revenue totals about 180M, roughly 43% of all-time company revenue of 421.34M. This does not prove double-counting, since attribution can legitimately cover a large share of sales. However, WhatsApp/CRM accounts for 58% of all attributed revenue on only 2M of spend, a return of roughly 50 times. That figure most likely reflects existing customers who were already buying, not new demand. This is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

> **Immediate action:** Validate WhatsApp/CRM attribution before scaling the channel. Measure whether its customers are new or returning, and run an incrementality test. Google is the strongest paid-media candidate on current figures, but its attributed revenue has not yet been tested for incrementality either.

## 6. Production Output and Bottlenecks
In 2026 to date, the five sewing lines planned about 1.67M units and produced about 1.52M, roughly 91% attainment. Every line falls between 88% and 94%, so the shortfall is systemic rather than confined to one line. Across all periods, attainment is about the same, at roughly 91% on 6.9M planned units.

About 82% of 2026 production orders record no primary bottleneck. Among the rest, finishing is the most frequent at about 11% of orders, while packing, printing, cutting, and sewing account for smaller shares. Sewing led the bottleneck record across all periods, but finishing has become the more visible constraint in 2026.

Because most orders record no bottleneck, the gap is probably driven by factors the bottleneck field does not capture. This points to a data-capture question as well as an operational one.

> **Immediate action:** Investigate the cause of the uniform shortfall across all lines rather than shifting capacity between them. Tighten bottleneck logging so that each delayed order records a cause, and review finishing capacity as the most common recorded constraint.

## 7. Cost of Poor Quality
The 2026 reject rate is 2.40%, with 21K units rejected and total cost of poor quality of 3.85M. The largest cost drivers by defect type are stitch defects (0.50M), size variance (0.47M), and finishing defects (0.45M), followed by fabric defects (0.43M). Print cracking and ink smudge are smaller in cost but still significant.

Root causes are spread evenly. Handling and packing lead at about 14%, followed by process variation, curing temperature, pattern and measurement, machine calibration, operator method, and material variation, each between 11% and 13%. No single cause dominates, so fixing one problem will not resolve the loss. The change has to come from a cross-functional quality process.

> **Immediate action:** Introduce inline quality checkpoints at sewing and finishing, focused on stitch and size checks. Pair these with a root-cause review that spans handling, process, and measurement rather than targeting one cause.

## 8. Downtime and Printing Performance
Printing machines recorded 1,340.6 hours of downtime in 2026. The largest reasons were setup and changeover (312 hours, 23%), mechanical faults (295 hours, 22%), and planned maintenance (284 hours, 21%). Material waiting accounted for 208 hours, operator shortage for 143 hours, and electrical faults for 99 hours.

Output is comparable across the five machines, ranging from about 0.28M to 0.33M printed units. Rework cost is about 200K, but total ink cost is 6.87M, which is large relative to the rework and rejection figures and deserves its own review.

Setup and planned maintenance together account for about 45% of downtime, and these are the most controllable causes.

> **Immediate action:** Target changeover time with standard work and setup reduction methods. Review the maintenance schedule against mechanical fault rates, and audit ink consumption against print volume.

---

## 9. Supply Chain and Materials
Average supplier lead time is 11.35 days. Material waiting accounts for about 16% of downtime hours, which links supplier reliability to factory output. Better supplier data would help separate material-driven delays from internal causes.

> **Immediate action:** Track supplier on-time delivery against material-wait downtime to identify which suppliers drive factory delays.

---

## 10. Priorities
The highest-value actions, in order, are these:
1. **First:** Stop margin leakage by replacing blanket homewear discounting with margin-gated promotions.
2. **Second:** Address wholesale margin, where a small improvement has the largest profit impact.
3. **Third:** Reduce quality losses and changeover downtime on the production floor.
4. **Fourth:** Reorder fast movers now and tie purchasing to the demand forecast.
5. **Fifth:** Validate marketing attribution before reallocating spend.
